Overview

In this lesson, we practice solving ratio word problems. The most important skill is not “plugging numbers in” — it’s learning how to translate a situation into parts, find the value of one part, and then solve.

Learning Targets

  • Translate word problems into ratio relationships
  • Identify the structure of a ratio problem (parts, total, difference)
  • Find the value of one part and use it to solve
  • Explain your reasoning clearly (not just an answer)

Key Vocabulary

  • Part — One share in a ratio (like “1 piece” of the ratio)
  • Total parts — The sum of all ratio parts
  • Difference — When a problem says “more than” or “less than”
  • Value of 1 part — How much each part is worth
  • Nested ratio — A ratio inside another ratio (multi-step splitting)

Big Idea

Ratio word problems become easier when you can: turn the story into parts and find the value of one part.

If you can find “1 part,” you can usually solve the whole problem.

Lesson Flow

Warm-Up

  • KenKen warm-up (focus + problem solving)
  • Quick “parts” review: total parts and value of 1 part

Activity: Ratio Detective (Whole-Class Build)

  • We solve a “difference” problem together (example: 65 more)
  • Focus: identify what the ratio represents, then find 1 part
  • You’ll help build the steps — not copy notes

Activity: Ratio Detective Stations

  • Work in small groups
  • Rotate through word problems (rope, rectangle, tickets, etc.)
  • At each station, you must label the problem type and show clear reasoning

Wrap-Up

  • Class reflection: What step makes ratio word problems easier?

How to Solve Ratio Word Problems

Use this checklist every time — especially before you start calculating.

  1. What does the ratio compare? (Who/what are the parts?)
  2. Find total parts (add the numbers in the ratio)
  3. Decide what the given number represents:
    • Total (all parts together)
    • One part (a single share)
    • Difference (how many more/less)
  4. Find the value of 1 part
  5. Answer the question (with units and a sentence)

Most mistakes happen when people skip Step 3.

What You Should Be Recording

  • The ratio you used (written clearly)
  • Total parts (and/or difference in parts, if needed)
  • What 1 part represents in the situation
  • Work shown neatly (so someone else could follow it)
  • A one-sentence explanation of your strategy

Green • Blue • Black Tasks

  • Green — Straightforward ratio problems (total or one part)
  • Blue — Difference problems (“more than / less than”) and multi-step
  • Black — Layered situations (nested ratios, equalizing, fractions + ratios)

Everyone starts with Green. Move on when you’re ready — challenge is about thinking, not speed.
